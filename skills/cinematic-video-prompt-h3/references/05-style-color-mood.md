# Artistic Style, Color & Mood

> **H3 mapping note (read first).** Style, color, and mood in H3 live inside the shot description. Three slots:
>
> 1. **Style descriptor (first words of `[Shot 1]`).** Sets the overall visual style for the whole video. Pick from the H3 allowed list: `Live-action,` / `cinematic,` / `2D-animated,` / `3D CG,` / `claymation,` / `watercolor,` / `vintage film,` (or any coherent style — see section A of this reference). For I2VA / FL2VA / L2VA / Ref2VA, derive the style from the reference image; for T2VA, pick from the brief. State style **once** at the top of `[Shot 1]` and repeat it in each shot if the brief risks style drift.
> 2. **Color palette / grading (trailing tag).** `Teal and orange color grading,` / `muted warm palette with lifted blacks,` / `cyberpunk neon palette,` / `split tone warm highlights cool shadows,`. Color should generally point the same direction as lighting.
> 3. **Mood (implied, not stated).** H3 reads `mood` as redundant with lighting + palette + subject expression. Drop the word `mood:` from your vocabulary. If you need to anchor it, fold into the lighting or color phrase (`low-key chiaroscuro lighting,` already reads as ominous; `muted warm palette,` already reads as nostalgic).
>
> **On style / lighting / color coordination.** A `Ghibli style,` + `hyperrealistic,` + `neon palette` mix is a contradiction and the model will average it into mush. **But deliberate counterpoint is a normal artistic tool** — a sunlit downpour, a warm palette in a grief scene, a cold blue room in a moment of joy. The audience reads contrast, not just harmony. When you intend contrast, name the source of the contrast so the model has a hook: `the warm afternoon sun falls on her face, the cold news in her hands,` / `rain catches the late sun and breaks into a thousand glints,`. The point isn't to ban contrasts; it's to avoid **unmotivated** contradictions.

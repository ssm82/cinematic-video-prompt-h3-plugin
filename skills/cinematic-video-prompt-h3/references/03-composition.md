# Composition

> **H3 mapping note (read first).** Composition applies to the **first frame** of `[Shot 1]` in H3 (and to every keyframe in I2VA / FL2VA / L2VA / Ref2VA). H3 reads composition as inline phrases inside the opening shot sentence:
>
> - Frame the subject: `framed at the rule-of-thirds intersection, …`
> - Position the subject: `placed left of center, …`, `centered in the frame, …`, `small in the wide frame to emphasize the landscape, …`
> - Use lines and depth: `with leading lines pulling toward the vanishing point, …`, `isolated in negative space, …`, `framed by a doorway in the foreground, …`
>
> Composition is **not** a separate field in H3 — it lives inside the shot description. For talking-head and product shots keep it minimal (`centered in the frame, soft bokeh background`); for landscape and fantasy it carries most of the storytelling.

Arranges elements inside the frame. Use for still images and the opening frame of any video clip.

## Mise-en-scène, Blocking & Spatial Continuity

> **Evidence status:** **Documented** for the named terms (industry-standard). **Heuristic** for which formulations H3-class models reproduce — describe the spatial layout in plain English ("she stands on screen left; he is on screen right") and the model reads it; abstract blocking jargon ("mise-en-scène", "staging in depth") is less reliably parsed.

The most common gap in cinematographic vocabulary is **what's actually happening inside the frame** — where the characters are, how they relate, where they look. Composition isn't only the first frame; it changes as actors and camera move.

### Mise-en-scène

| Term | Definition | H3 phrasing |
|---|---|---|
| Mise-en-scène | Everything inside the frame: set, costume, light, actor placement, their relationships | `mise-en-scène — the cramped studio apartment, the single hanging bulb, the man at the easel, the woman watching from the doorway, …` |

### Blocking

| Term | Definition | H3 phrasing |
|---|---|---|
| Blocking | Where actors stand, how they move, where they stop, who faces whom | describe the placement and movement: `she stands in the foreground on screen left; he remains by the door in the background on screen right. She steps aside, revealing the suitcase behind her, …` |
| Staging in Depth | Action distributed across foreground, midground, background | `staging in depth — the child plays in the foreground, the mother works at the table in the midground, the father reads by the window in the background, …` |
| Reveal through Blocking | Information disclosed by actor movement, not camera movement | `she steps aside, revealing the figure behind her, …` |
| Business / Prop Interaction | An actor handles a specific object to give the hands something to do | `business — she twists the ring on her finger, sets down the teacup, picks up the letter, …` |
| Cross (actor movement) | An actor moves from one side of frame to the other | `she crosses from screen right to screen left in three steps, …` |

### Screen direction and eyeline

> **180° rule and screen direction are inseparable.** The two characters in a conversation keep their screen-side relationship across cuts so the audience reads the geography. State the rule once at the start of the dialogue sequence, then keep both characters on the agreed sides.

| Term | Definition | H3 phrasing |
|---|---|---|
| Screen Direction | Which side of the frame a character enters from / exits to / faces | state explicitly: `she enters from screen right and exits screen left, …` / `he faces screen left, his eyeline aimed off-frame, …` |
| Eyeline | Where a character is looking — their gaze direction | `her eyeline is just past his left shoulder, …` / `he looks down at the letter, …` |
| Eyeline Match | A cut from character looking to what they see | covered in `references/08-editing-transitions.md` |
| Action Axis / Line of Action | The imaginary line between two subjects; the camera stays on one side to keep screen direction consistent | state the axis at the start of the dialogue: `the conversation stays on the woman's right side; both shots keep the camera on her right, …` (the 180° rule expressed as a screen relationship) |
| Off-screen Space | The world beyond the frame edge — implied but not visible | `the gesture points off-screen left, the audience inferring what she sees, …` / `a knock comes from off-screen — she turns toward it, …` |
| Looking Off-screen | Subject looks toward something the audience can't see | `she looks off-screen left, her face unreadable, …` |
| Subjective Camera / POV | Camera represents the character's gaze | covered in `references/01-camera-angles-and-movement.md` |
| Cutting on Look | A cut triggered by where the character looks | `the cut happens on her look — the camera cuts to what she sees, …` |

### Continuity across cuts

| Term | Definition | H3 phrasing |
|---|---|---|
| Spatial Continuity | Screen direction, eyeline, and object placement stay consistent across cuts | state the rule once and stay consistent |
| Neutral Shot / On-axis Shot | A shot taken directly on the axis of action — used to re-orient the audience | `a neutral on-axis shot re-establishes the geography: both characters frame-center, equal weight, …` |
| Spatial Re-establishment | A new wide or master shot inserted after cuts have tightened in | `a spatial re-establishment — at MM:SS.mmm, the camera cuts back to the wide shot, the geography refreshed, …` |
| Master Shot | A wide shot containing the whole scene | `a master shot — the wide frames both characters and the room, the whole scene visible, …` |
| Coverage | The set of additional shots (mediums, close-ups) that the editor uses to break up the master | `the master shot is followed by coverage: a medium of him, a close-up of her, an insert of the letter, …` |
| Clean Single | A single on a character with no other person in frame | `a clean single on him, no part of her visible, …` |
| Dirty Single | A single with part of another character in the foreground | `a dirty single on her, his shoulder in soft focus in the foreground, …` |
| Over-the-Shoulder (OTS) | A shot taken over one character's shoulder, looking at the other | `an over-the-shoulder shot from his shoulder, framing her face, …` |
| OTS Matching | Across two OTS shots, the foreground shoulder and the framed face mirror each other | describe both halves: `her shoulder fills the foreground left; he faces screen right. Reverse: his shoulder fills the foreground right; she faces screen left. …` |

### Reframing and the camera-relative frame

Composition is **not** only the first frame — it changes as the actors and camera move.

| Term | Definition | H3 phrasing |
|---|---|---|
| Reframing | Adjusting the framing to keep the subject well-placed as they move | `the camera reframes — she steps forward, the framing tightens to keep her face at the upper third, …` |
| Hold / Settle | A moment where the composition stays still (camera and actors stop) | `the camera holds on the door; she settles into stillness, the composition frozen for a beat, …` |
| Reveal / Conceal | A movement that exposes or hides information within the frame | `a foreground figure conceals her face, then steps aside to reveal her, …` |
| Foreground Occlusion | Something in the foreground partially blocks the subject | `a tree branch in the foreground occludes her face as she passes, …` |
| Start Frame / End Frame | The composition at the beginning and end of a moving shot | describe both: `start frame: the door at center. End frame: her face at the upper-right thirds intersection. …` |
| Camera-relative Motion | Subject moves toward / away from / across the camera | `she walks toward the camera, her face growing larger, …` / `he crosses past the camera, exit screen right, …` |
| Walking Toward / Walking Away | Subject depth changes (size in frame) | `she walks toward the camera, her face filling the frame by the end of the shot, …` |
| Walking Across Frame | Subject moves laterally; depth stays constant | `he crosses the frame from screen left to screen right, …` |
| Walking Stationary | Subject moves in place (depth holds) | `she walks in place — her feet stepping but her position in the frame held, …` |

### Workable blocking sentence (recipe)

A blocking beat fits in one or two sentences. The model reads them as literal positions and movements.

```
She stands in the foreground on screen left, her back to the camera.
He remains by the door in the background on screen right, facing her.
At 00:02.000, she turns toward the door. At 00:04.000, she walks
toward him, closing the distance. She stops one pace short. Her eyeline
lifts to his.
```

Note: the example uses screen left / screen right (not "her left" / "his right") so the model reads them as frame coordinates, not body coordinates. Keep that distinction throughout the description.
